1.1 Inner Products, Orthogonality, and Orthogonal Polynomials

[Textbook references: §3.6]

You may recall from linear algebra that two vectors, 𝒙,𝒚∈ℝn are orthogonal if their dot product is zero, 𝒙⋅𝒚=0. In this case, the two vectors meet at right angles. The concept of orthogonality (meeting at right angles) is of central importance, and can be generalized from vectors in ℝn with Euclidean geometry, to abstract vector spaces using the concept of an inner product.

Note that the dot product 𝒙⋅𝒚:=∑i=1nxi⁢yi satisfies the following three properties:

  1. 1.

    Positivity: 𝒙⋅𝒙=∑i=1nxi2=‖x‖2>0, except when 𝒙=𝟎 (in which case 𝒙⋅𝒙=0).

  2. 2.

    Symmetry: 𝒙⋅𝒚=𝒚⋅𝒙.

  3. 3.

    Linearity: (a⁢𝒙+𝒚)⋅𝒛=a⁢(𝒙⋅𝒛)+𝒚⋅𝒛.

These are exactly the three products we will use to define an inner product.

Definition 1.

Let V be a vector space. An inner product is a function ⟨⋅,⋅⟩:V×V→ℝ satisfying the following three properties:

  1. 1.

    Positivity: ⟨u,u⟩≥0, with equality if and only if u=0.

  2. 2.

    Symmetry: ⟨u,v⟩=⟨v,u⟩.

  3. 3.

    Linearity: ⟨a⁢u+v,w⟩=a⁢⟨u,w⟩+⟨v,w⟩.

Inner products are closely related to the concept of norms, which we have already worked with.

Definition 2.

A norm is a function ‖c⁢o⁢d⁢t‖:V→ℝ satisfying:

  1. 1.

    Positivity: ‖u‖≥0, with equality if and only if u=0.

  2. 2.

    Absolute homogeneity: ‖a⁢u‖=|a|⁢‖u‖.

  3. 3.

    Triangle inequality: ‖u+v‖≤‖u‖+‖v‖.

Any inner product induces a norm, meaning that if, given an inner product ⟨,⟩, we can define

‖u‖:=⟨u,u⟩,

then ∥⋅∥ will automatically satisfy the axioms of a norm. The dot product induces the Euclidean norm.

Definition 3.

Two vectors u,v∈V are orthogonal with respect to the inner product ⟨⋅,⋅⟩ if ⟨u,v⟩=0.

To give an example of inner products and norms that will be useful to use, we will consider V=ℙn for some n on the interval [−1,1]. We saw before that we can measure the “size” of a function using the max norm. We will now introduce a different measure of the size of a function, using the norm induced by an inner product.

Define the Legendre inner product (also commonly known as the L2 inner product) by

⟨p,q⟩=∫−11p⁢(x)⁢q⁢(x)⁢𝑑x.

This definition satisfies the three conditions of an inner product. (Why?) It induces the following norm, known as the L2 norm,

‖p‖L2=⟨p,p⟩=(∫−11p⁢(x)2⁢𝑑x)1/2.

Just like vectors in ℝn can be orthogonal to each other, polynomials in ℙn can also be orthogonal to each other with respect to an inner product.

Consider ℙn and an inner product ⟨⋅,⋅⟩ (which may be the L2 inner product, or may be a different inner product). A family of orthogonal polynomials is a set {p0,p1,…,pn} such that

deg⁡(pk)=k,and⟨pi,pj⟩=0⁢for⁢i≠j.

Such a family is always linearly independent, and therefore forms a basis for ℙn. Such a family is unique up to a scaling of each polynomial. Therefore, they can be chosen uniquely to be orthonormal, meaning that ⟨pi,pj⟩=δi⁢j.

We can see that for any q∈ℙm, m<n, q can be written as a linear combination of {p0,…,pm}, and therefore

⟨pn,q⟩=0for all q∈ℙm.

If the inner product satisfies ⟨x⁢f,g⟩=⟨f,x⁢g⟩ for all f and g in the space, then the orthogonal polynomials satisfy a three-term recurrence (item 4 of Textbook Theorem 3.38). Given p0 and p1, we have

pn+1⁢(x)=(An⁢x−Bn)⁢pn⁢(x)−Cn⁢pn−1⁢(x),n≥1,

for some constants An, Bn, and Cn.

To see this, expand x⁢pn in the orthogonal basis {p0,…,pn+1} for ℙn+1. The coefficient of pj is ⟨x⁢pn,pj⟩/⟨pj,pj⟩. For 0≤j≤n−2, we have

⟨x⁢pn,pj⟩=⟨pn,x⁢pj⟩=0,

since x⁢pj∈ℙn−1. Thus x⁢pn is a linear combination of pn−1, pn, and pn+1. Since x⁢pn has degree n+1, the coefficient of pn+1 is nonzero, so we can solve for pn+1 to obtain the recurrence. For the Legendre inner product, the required identity follows directly from (x⁢f)⁢g=f⁢(x⁢g) inside the integral.

The orthogonal polynomials with respect to the Legendre inner-product are the Legendre polynomials, which can be given by

P0⁢(x)=1,P1⁢(x)=x,Pn+1⁢(x)=2⁢n+1n+1⁢x⁢Pn⁢(x)−nn+1⁢Pn−1⁢(x),n≥1.

In the three-term recurrence, An=(2⁢n+1)/(n+1), Bn=0, and Cn=n/(n+1). The next three polynomials are

P2⁢(x)=3⁢x2−12,P3⁢(x)=5⁢x3−3⁢x2,P4⁢(x)=35⁢x4−30⁢x2+38.

1 Numerical Integration and Differentiation: Basic Quadrature

[Textbook reference: §4.1]

We previously saw the midpoint rule for approximating integrations,

∫abf⁢(x)⁢𝑑x=(b−a)⁢f⁢(a+b2)+𝒪⁢((b−a)3),

for f∈C2⁢[a,b]. This is one example of numerical quadrature, which is a term that refers to the approximation of integrals through computable finite sums. We have focused on polynomial approximation for good reason. Most of the quadratures we will considered will be based on the idea of approximation f first by a polynomial p, and then integrating p exactly, so that ∫f≈∫p.

Definition 1 (Quadrature; Textbook Definition 4.1).

For f∈C⁢[a,b], a quadrature approximation of I⁢(f)=∫abf⁢(x)⁢𝑑x is a linear combination of values of f at distinct points {x0,x1,…,xn}⊂[a,b],

I⁢(f)≈Q⁢(f)=∑j=0nwj⁢f⁢(xj).

We refer to the nodes xj as quadrature points, and the numbers wj as quadrature weights. We say that a quadrature Q is exact on ℙm when Q⁢(p)=I⁢(p) for all p∈ℙm.

Lemma 1 (Textbook Lemma 4.2).

Both I and Q are linear functionals on C⁢[a,b]. In other words, for all f,g∈C⁢[a,b] and α,β∈ℝ,

I⁢(α⁢f+β⁢g)=α⁢I⁢(f)+β⁢I⁢(g)andQ⁢(α⁢f+β⁢g)=α⁢Q⁢(f)+β⁢Q⁢(g).

Consequently, Q is exact on ℙm if and only if Q⁢(pj)=I⁢(pj) for every element of a basis {p0,…,pm} of ℙm.

Definition 2 (Interpolatory and Newton–Cotes Quadrature; Textbook Definition 4.3).

Given f∈C⁢[a,b] and distinct quadrature points {x0,x1,…,xn}⊂[a,b], the corresponding interpolatory quadrature is obtained by integrating the Lagrange interpolating polynomial p∈ℙn exactly:

Q⁢(f)=∫abp⁢(x)⁢𝑑x=∑j=0nwj⁢f⁢(xj),wherewj=∫abℓj⁢(x)⁢𝑑x,

where {ℓj} are the Lagrange polynomials for the quadrature points. By construction, an interpolatory quadrature is exact on ℙn. In the special case that the quadrature points are evenly spaced,

xj=a+jn⁢(b−a),0≤j≤n,

the rule is called a (closed) Newton–Cotes quadrature.

Lemma 2 (Textbook Definition 4.3).

Any (n+1)-point quadrature that is exact on ℙm for some m≥n must be the interpolatory quadrature for its quadrature points.

Proof.

Let {xi} denote the quadrature points. Since Q is exact, Q⁢(ℓj)=∫abℓj⁢(x)⁢𝑑x. By the definition of Q,

Q⁢(ℓj) =∑k=0nwk⁢ℓj⁢(xk)=∑k=0nwk⁢δj⁢k=wj,

and so wj=∫abℓj⁢(x)⁢𝑑x, implying that Q is interpolatory. ∎

Theorem 1 (Textbook Theorem 4.4).

Suppose that an (n+1)-point quadrature Q is exact on ℙm for some m≥n. If q∈ℙm satisfies q⁢(xj)=f⁢(xj) for 0≤j≤n, then

I⁢(f)−Q⁢(f)=I⁢(f−q).
Proof.

By the previous lemma, if a quadrature Q of the form (4.1) is exact on ℙm for some m≥n, then it is necessarily an interpolatory quadrature (4.2). Since q interpolates f at the quadrature points, and Q is exact on ℙm, we have I⁢(f)−Q⁢(f)=I⁢(f)−Q⁢(q)=I⁢(f)−I⁢(q)=I⁢(f−q). ∎

The above theorem implies that the quadrature error is given by the integral of the interpolation error. Therefore, our previous results bounding the interpolation error can be used to derive error estimates for quadratures.

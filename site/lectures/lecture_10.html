1 Numerical Integration and Differentiation: Basic Quadrature

[Textbook reference: §4.1]

We previously saw the midpoint rule for approximating integrations,

∫abf⁢(x)⁢𝑑x=(b−a)⁢f⁢(a+b2)+𝒪⁢((b−a)3),

for f∈C2⁢[a,b]. This is one example of numerical quadrature, which is a term that refers to the approximation of integrals through computable finite sums. We have focused on polynomial approximation for good reason. Most of the quadratures we will considered will be based on the idea of approximation f first by a polynomial p, and then integrating p exactly, so that ∫f≈∫p.

Definition 1 (Quadrature; Textbook Definition 4.1).

For f∈C⁢[a,b], a quadrature approximation of I⁢(f)=∫abf⁢(x)⁢𝑑x is a linear combination of values of f at distinct points {x0,x1,…,xn}⊂[a,b],

I⁢(f)≈Q⁢(f)=∑j=0nwj⁢f⁢(xj).

We refer to the nodes xj as quadrature points, and the numbers wj as quadrature weights. We say that a quadrature Q is exact on ℙm when Q⁢(p)=I⁢(p) for all p∈ℙm.

Lemma 1 (Textbook Lemma 4.2).

Both I and Q are linear functionals on C⁢[a,b]. In other words, for all f,g∈C⁢[a,b] and α,β∈ℝ,

I⁢(α⁢f+β⁢g)=α⁢I⁢(f)+β⁢I⁢(g)andQ⁢(α⁢f+β⁢g)=α⁢Q⁢(f)+β⁢Q⁢(g).

Consequently, Q is exact on ℙm if and only if Q⁢(pj)=I⁢(pj) for every element of a basis {p0,…,pm} of ℙm.

Definition 2 (Interpolatory and Newton–Cotes Quadrature; Textbook Definition 4.3).

Given f∈C⁢[a,b] and distinct quadrature points {x0,x1,…,xn}⊂[a,b], the corresponding interpolatory quadrature is obtained by integrating the Lagrange interpolating polynomial p∈ℙn exactly:

Q⁢(f)=∫abp⁢(x)⁢𝑑x=∑j=0nwj⁢f⁢(xj),wherewj=∫abℓj⁢(x)⁢𝑑x,

where {ℓj} are the Lagrange polynomials for the quadrature points. By construction, an interpolatory quadrature is exact on ℙn. In the special case that the quadrature points are evenly spaced,

xj=a+jn⁢(b−a),0≤j≤n,

the rule is called a (closed) Newton–Cotes quadrature.

Lemma 2 (Textbook Definition 4.3).

Any (n+1)-point quadrature that is exact on ℙm for some m≥n must be the interpolatory quadrature for its quadrature points.

Proof.

Let {xi} denote the quadrature points. Since Q is exact, Q⁢(ℓj)=∫abℓj⁢(x)⁢𝑑x. By the definition of Q,

Q⁢(ℓj) =∑k=0nwk⁢ℓj⁢(xk)=∑k=0nwk⁢δj⁢k=wj,

and so wj=∫abℓj⁢(x)⁢𝑑x, implying that Q is interpolatory. ∎

Theorem 1 (Textbook Theorem 4.4).

Suppose that an (n+1)-point quadrature Q is exact on ℙm for some m≥n. If q∈ℙm satisfies q⁢(xj)=f⁢(xj) for 0≤j≤n, then

I⁢(f)−Q⁢(f)=I⁢(f−q).
Proof.

By the previous lemma, if a quadrature Q of the form (4.1) is exact on ℙm for some m≥n, then it is necessarily an interpolatory quadrature (4.2). Since q interpolates f at the quadrature points, and Q is exact on ℙm, we have I⁢(f)−Q⁢(f)=I⁢(f)−Q⁢(q)=I⁢(f)−I⁢(q)=I⁢(f−q). ∎

The above theorem implies that the quadrature error is given by the integral of the interpolation error. Therefore, our previous results bounding the interpolation error can be used to derive error estimates for quadratures.

The trapezoid rule and Simpson’s rule are examples of Newton–Cotes quadrature rules. We can derive the (n+1)-point Newton–Cotes rule for any n by forming the degree-n Lagrange interpolant at equispaced nodes, and then computing its integral. We saw that using equispaced nodes for polynomial interpolation could result in very large errors, for example when interpolating the Runge function. This is known as the Runge phenomenon. We may ask whether the Runge phenomenon will then cause degraded accuracy of the Newton–Cotes rules, and we will say that the answer is yes: for large n, the accuracy of Newton–Cotes rules can be disastrously bad. Just like we were able to “fix” the issue of polynomial interpolation by using a different set of interpolation nodes (Chebyshev was the example we saw), we can improve the accuracy of interpolatory quadrature by using different quadrature points.

Another approach is to partition the interval [a,b] into subintervals, and use a “basic quadrature rule” (like one of the ones from the table) to integrate the function over each subinterval. Then, since the integral over [a,b] is the sum of the integral over the subintervals, it can be approximated by the sum of the subinterval quadrature approximations. This is called composite quadrature. We expect that as the number of subintervals grows (and so the size of each subinterval shrinks), the composite quadrature approximation will improve. Indeed, we see from Theorem 2 that the quadrature error scales like the width of the interval raised to some power (at least 2 in all of the examples from the table). This implies that as we add more subintervals, the error of the composite quadrature will decrease (why?).

To partition [a,b], we let

a=x0<x1<⋯<xn=b,

giving the subintervals [xj−1,xj]. Let hj=xj−xj−1 denote the width of the jth interval, and let yj=(xj−1+xj)/2 denote its midpoint. Very commonly, the points xj will be equispaced, which means that hj≡h=(b−a)/n for all j.

In practice, the composite versions of simple rules are much more commonly used than the simple rules by themselves (since if b−a is large, the simple rules will not give a good approximation), and so often we will use e.g. “trapezoid rule” or “midpoint rule” to refer to “composite trapezoid rule” or “composite midpoint rule”. 1n⁢∑j=1nf(4)⁢(ξj)=f(4)⁢(ξ)

Note that the power of h that appears in the composite rule is one lower than the power of h that appears in the basic rule. This is because n∼1/h, and the composite rule is obtained by summing n basic quadrature approximations, so we expect the error over the entire interval to be roughly n times as large as the error over a single subinterval. This same reasoning applies to all of the composite rules.

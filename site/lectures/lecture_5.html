1 Solving Equations and Root Finding

1.1 Newton and Secant Methods

[Textbook reference: §2.2]

Recall that the regula falsi method used the root of the secant line to find the next approximation to the root of f⁢(x). The idea is that the secant line can be thought of as an approximation to f⁢(x), and its zero is easily computable. This same idea of linear approximation underlies both of the methods that we will see in this section: Newton’s method and the secant method.

These methods are not root bracketing methods. They do not require that we start with an interval that contains the root, but they also do not guarantee that we obtain nested intervals containing the root. In fact, these methods may sometimes fail to converge. However, when they do converge, they usually do so much more rapidly than the methods we have considered earlier. This can be seen as a trade-off between robustness and speed of convergence. We briefly note that there are ways to combine these fast methods with robust root bracketing methods to obtain methods that are both fast and robust. The combination of the secant method with bisection is called “Brent’s method”, and the combination of Newton’s method with bisection is often called “Newt-safe”.

1.1.1 Newton’s Method

Newton’s method starts with an “initial guess” x0, which should be “close” to a root r of f⁢(x), and then proceeds by approximating f⁢(x) by its linearization, i.e. first-order Taylor polynomial taken at the point x0,

p1⁢(x)=f⁢(x0)+f′⁢(x0)⁢(x−x0).

Then, the root of f⁢(x) is approximated by the root of p1⁢(x),

f′⁢(x0)⁢x=f′⁢(x0)⁢x0−f⁢(x0)⟹x=x0−f⁢(x0)f′⁢(x0).

We call this new point x1. The hope is that x1 is a better approximation than x0 (i.e. closer to the true root r). Then, if we repeat this process, starting now at x1, we will get an even better approximation x2, and so on. This gives the iteration:

xn+1=xn−f⁢(xn)f′⁢(xn),

(as long as f′⁢(xn)≠0).

The secant method is very similar. Instead of approximating f⁢(x) by its first-order Taylor polynomial, we approximate it by its secant line through two points (x0,f⁢(x0)) and (x1,f⁢(x1)). This requires two initial guesses, x0≠x1. Then, the root of the secant line is given by

x2=x1−x1−x0f⁢(x1)−f⁢(x0)⁢f⁢(x1)=x0⁢f⁢(x1)−x1⁢f⁢(x0)f⁢(x1)−f⁢(x0).

Then, we proceed by using the most recent two points for the secant line, obtaining the iteration

xn+1=xn−xn−xn−1f⁢(xn)−f⁢(xn−1)⁢f⁢(xn)=xn−1⁢f⁢(xn)−xn⁢f⁢(xn−1)f⁢(xn)−f⁢(xn−1).

The secant method requires evaluation only of the function f, whereas Newton’s method requires evaluation of the function f and its derivative f′.

We summarize Newton’s method and the secant method in the algorithms below.

1:function Newton(f,f′,x,ftol,xtol,maxit)
2:     for n=1,2,…,maxit do
3:         δ←f⁢(x)/f′⁢(x)
4:         x←x−δ
5:         if |f⁢(x)|≤ftol or |δ|≤xtol then
6:              return x
7:         end if
8:     end for
9:     Did not converge.
10:end function
1:function Secant(f,x0,x1,ftol,xtol,maxit)
2:     for n=1,2,…,maxit do
3:         δ←(x1−x0)⁢f⁢(x1)/(f⁢(x1)−f⁢(x0))
4:         x0←x1,  x1←x1−δ
5:         if |f⁢(x1)|≤ftol or |δ|≤xtol then
6:              return x1
7:         end if
8:     end for
9:     Did not converge.
10:end function

1.1.2 Differences Between Regula Falsi and Secant Method

Both the regula falsi and the secant methods use the root of the secant line as the next point in the iteration, and so they appear to be very similar. However, there is a key difference: the secant method always uses the newest two points to form the secant line. Regula falsi, on the other hand, uses the secant line between the newest point and one of the previous points, chosen so that f⁢(x) has opposite sign at the two endpoints (ensuring that the root is bracketed). Other than this difference, the methods are the same. Although this difference appears minor, it can have significant implications for robustness and speed of convergence. Regula falsi guarantees that the root remains bracketed, and is guaranteed to converge as long the starting interval is valid, but convergence may be slow. Heuristically, secant method should converge faster because it is always using the most up-to-date information: we expect xn→r, so the most recent points xn and xn−1 should be the best approximations so far to the root r, rather than using an or bn as one of the endpoints, which may be “stale” information. The secant method does indeed converge (much) faster than regula falsi, and we will make this argument rigorous shortly. The drawback is that it is not guaranteed to converge even if the initial two points bracket the root. Additional criteria are needed to guarantee convergence, and these generally depend on unknown parameters that cannot be checked practically.

Theorem 1 (Convergence of Newton’s method; Textbook Theorem 2.12).

Suppose that r∈(a,b) is a root of f∈C2⁢(a,b), and f′⁢(r)≠0. There is a δ>0 such that, if x0∈(r−δ,r+δ), then the Newton iterates {xn} will be well-defined and in (r−δ,r+δ) for n≥0, and xn→r at least quadratically, with

xn+1−r(xn−r)2→f′′⁢(r)2⁢f′⁢(r)as n→∞,

provided xn≠r for any n. If f′′⁢(r)≠0, the convergence of Newton’s method is precisely quadratic.

Proof.

Let xn∈(a,b) with f′⁢(xn)≠0. Let p be the tangent line through (xn,f⁢(xn)), i.e. the first-order Taylor polynomial of f at xn,

p⁢(x)=f⁢(xn)+f′⁢(xn)⁢(x−xn).

Taylor’s Theorem gives

f⁢(x)=p⁢(x)+f′′⁢(ξ)2⁢(x−xn)2

for some ξ between xn and x. Evaluating at the root r then gives

−p⁢(r)=f′′⁢(ξn)2⁢(r−xn)2

where ξn is between xn and r. The next point xn+1 in Newton’s method is the root of p, i.e. p⁢(xn+1)=0. So,

−p⁢(r) =p⁢(xn+1)−p⁢(r)
=f⁢(xn)+f′⁢(xn)⁢(xn+1−xn)−f⁢(xn)−f′⁢(xn)⁢(r−xn)
=f′⁢(xn)⁢(xn+1−r).

Equating these two gives

xn+1−r=f′′⁢(ξn)2⁢f′⁢(xn)⁢(xn−r)2. (1)

We claim that the ratio f′′⁢(ξn)2⁢f′⁢(xn) is bounded as long as xn is sufficiently close to the root r. To see this, note that since f∈C2⁢(a,b), f′′⁢(x) is bounded above close to r. Additionally, since f′ is continuous in (a,b) and f′⁢(r)≠0, f′⁢(x) is bounded below close to r. Therefore, there exists some δ>0 such that

C≔maxx∈[r−δ,r+δ]⁡|f′′⁢(x)|2⁢minx∈[r−δ,r+δ]⁡|f′⁢(x)|<∞.

We can choose δ even smaller to ensure γ≔C⁢δ<1. Then, if xn∈(r−δ,r+δ), (1) gives

|xn+1−r|≤C⁢|xn−r|2≤γ⁢|xn−r|<|xn−r|<δ, (2)

so xn+1∈(r−δ,r+δ) as well. This means that if x0∈(r−δ,r+δ), then xn will be for n=1,2,…. Let en=xn−r. From (2),

|en|≤γn⁢|e0|⁢γn⁢(b−a).

Since γ<1, this implies |en|→0, and so xn→r. The remainder of the theorem statements follow from (1). ∎

1 Finite Difference Approximations of Derivatives

[Textbook reference: §4.2]

Calculus has two basic operations: integration and differentiation. In the previous lecture, we saw some numerical methods, called quadrature rules, for approximating integrals. In this lecture, we will focus on approximating derivatives. Just like numerical quadrature took weighted combinations of function values at quadrature points to approximate the integral, we will take weighted combinations of function values at certain points to approximate the kth derivative, obtaining a finite difference approximation.

Definition 1 (Finite Difference Schemes; Textbook Definition 4.10).

Let {x0,x1,…,xn} be distinct nodes, c a point, and f a function that is k-times differentiable at c. A finite difference approximation of f(k)⁢(c) is a weighted sum of the values of f at the nodes,

f(k)⁢(c)≈Dk⁢(f)=∑j=0nwj(k)⁢f⁢(xj). (1)

We say that Dk is exact on ℙm as an approximation of the kth derivative at c when Dk⁢(f)=f(k)⁢(c) for all f∈ℙm.

Although not used in the textbook, the nodes {xj} are often collectively referred to as the stencil.

The interpolatory finite difference scheme associated with these nodes is Dk⁢(f)=p(k)⁢(c), where p∈ℙn satisfies p⁢(xj)=f⁢(xj) for 0≤j≤n. For an interpolatory difference scheme,

wj(k)=ℓj(k)⁢(c), (2)

where ℓ0,…,ℓn are the Lagrange polynomials associated with these nodes.

Note that an interpolatory finite difference scheme using n+1 points is automatically exact on ℙn. As with quadrature, the converse statement is also true.

Lemma 1.

If a finite difference approximation of the form (1) is exact on ℙm for m≥n, then it is interpolatory.

Proof.

Since the approximation is exact, Dk⁢(ℓj)=ℓj(k)⁢(c). But,

Dk⁢(ℓj)=∑i=0nwi(k)⁢ℓj⁢(xi)=∑i=0nwi(k)⁢δi⁢j=wj(k),

proving (2). ∎

Theorem 1 (Interpolant Differentiation Theorem; Textbook Theorem 4.11).

Suppose that the finite difference scheme (1) for the kth derivative at c is exact on ℙm for some m≥n≥k. Assume that f is k-times differentiable at c and is defined at the nodes. If q∈ℙm satisfies q⁢(xj)=f⁢(xj) for 0≤j≤n, then

f(k)⁢(c)−Dk⁢(f)=f(k)⁢(c)−q(k)⁢(c).
Proof.

Since q interpolates f at the nodes, we have

Dk⁢(f)=∑j=0nwj(k)⁢f⁢(xj)=∑j=0nwj(k)⁢q⁢(xj)=Dk⁢(q).

Since q∈ℙm and Dk is exact on ℙm, we have Dk⁢(q)=q(k)⁢(c). Therefore,

f(k)⁢(c)−Dk⁢(f)=f(k)⁢(c)−Dk⁢(q)=f(k)⁢(c)−q(k)⁢(c),

as desired. ∎

This theorem allows us to analyze the accuracy of finite difference approximations using the Lagrange interpolation error identities. We can also use a different technique, based on Taylor’s theorem, to obtain error estimates.

Theorem 2 (Textbook Theorem 4.12).

Suppose that the difference scheme (1) is exact on ℙm as an approximation of the kth derivative at c for some m≥n≥k, and that f∈Cm+1⁢[a,b] on some interval [a,b] containing {c,x0,x1,…,xn}. Then

|f(k)⁢(c)−Dk⁢(f)|≤‖f(m+1)‖C⁢[a,b](m+1)!⁢∑j=0n|wj(k)⁢(xj−c)m+1|.
Proof.

By Taylor’s theorem, we have

f⁢(x)=∑ℓ=0mf(ℓ)⁢(c)ℓ!⁢(x−c)ℓ⏟T⁢(x)+f(m+1)⁢(ξ⁢(x))(m+1)!⁢(x−c)m+1⏟R⁢(x)=T⁢(x)+R⁢(x).

Note also that

f(k)⁢(c)=T(k)⁢(c)+R(k)⁢(c)=T(k)⁢(c),

and so, by exactness of the approximation, Dk⁢(T)=T(k)⁢(c)=f(k)⁢(c). Therefore, Dk⁢(f)=Dk⁢(T)+Dk⁢(R)=f(k)⁢(c)+Dk⁢(R), and so it remains to estimate Dk⁢(R), which is given by

Dk⁢(R)=∑j=0nwj(k)⁢f(m+1)⁢(ξ⁢(xj))(m+1)!⁢(xj−c)m+1,

from which the result follows. ∎

We often consider finite difference approximations parameterized by h, which represents the distance between the nodes. We will see that the weights wj(k) are proportional to h−k and (xj−c)m+1 is proportional to hm+1, and so

|f(k)⁢(c)−Dk⁢(f)|=𝒪⁢(hm+1−k).
Example 1 (Centered difference for f′⁢(c); Textbook Example 4.13).

Let h>0, and take x0=c−h and x1=c+h. The Lagrange polynomials are

ℓ0⁢(x)=x−(c+h)−2⁢h,ℓ1⁢(x)=x−(c−h)2⁢h.

Differentiating at c gives the centered difference approximation

D1⁢(f)=ℓ0′⁢(c)⁢f⁢(c−h)+ℓ1′⁢(c)⁢f⁢(c+h)=f⁢(c+h)−f⁢(c−h)2⁢h.

By construction, D1 is exact on ℙ1. For r⁢(x)=(x−c)2 and s⁢(x)=(x−c)3, we have

D1⁢(r)=0=r′⁢(c),D1⁢(s)=h2≠s′⁢(c).

Adjoining r to a basis of ℙ1 gives a basis of ℙ2, so D1 is exact on ℙ2 but not on ℙ3.

Suppose that f∈C3⁢[c−h,c+h]. Since D1 is exact on ℙ2, we apply Theorem 4.12 with m=2. Using the weights −1/(2⁢h) and 1/(2⁢h), we obtain

|f′⁢(c)−D1⁢(f)|≤‖f′′′‖C⁢[c−h,c+h]3!⁢(h32⁢h+h32⁢h)=‖f′′′‖C⁢[c−h,c+h]6⁢h2.

Let h>0, and take x0=c−h, x1=c, and x2=c+h. The Lagrange polynomials are

ℓ0⁢(x) =(x−c)⁢(x−(c+h))2⁢h2,
ℓ1⁢(x) =(x−(c−h))⁢(x−(c+h))−h2,

D2⁢(f)=ℓ0′′⁢(c)⁢f⁢(c−h)+ℓ1′′⁢(c)⁢f⁢(c)+ℓ2′′⁢(c)⁢f⁢(c+h)=f⁢(c−h)−2⁢f⁢(c)+f⁢(c+h)h2.

Example 3 (Interpolatory weights for the first derivative; Textbook Example 4.14).

Suppose that c=xi is one of the nodes. Recall that the Lagrange polynomials are

ℓj⁢(x)=λj⁢∏k≠j(x−xk),λj=1∏k≠j(xj−xk).

By the product rule,

ℓj′⁢(x)=λj⁢∑s≠j∏k≠j,s(x−xk).

Evaluating at xi≠xj, all terms in the sum vanish, except for s=i, giving

ℓj′⁢(xi)=λj⁢∏k≠i,j(xi−xk)=λjλi⁢(xi−xj).

For j=i, we have

ℓi′⁢(xi)=∑s≠iλi⁢∏k≠i,s(xi−xk)=∑s≠i1xi−xs.

Therefore, the weights wj(1) are given by

wj(1)=ℓj′⁢(xi)={λjλi⁢(xi−xj),j≠i,∑s≠i1xi−xs,j=i.

For equispaced nodes xj=x0+j⁢h with h>0, the barycentric weights are proportional to (−1)j⁢(nj). Substituting these weights and xi−xj=(i−j)⁢h gives textbook equation (4.13),

wj(1)={(nj)(ni)⁢(−1)i−j(i−j)⁢h,j≠i,1h⁢∑s≠i1i−s,j=i.

Theorem 3 (Vandermonde Approach to Finite Difference Weights; Textbook Theorem 4.15).

Let {x0,…,xn} be distinct nodes, and let c be a point. Define the shifted Vandermonde matrix V and the diagonal matrix S by

V=(1x0−c⋯(x0−c)n1x1−c⋯(x1−c)n⋮⋮⋱⋮1xn−c⋯(xn−c)n),S=(0!1!⋱n!).

The matrix W with entries Wk⁢j=wj(k)=ℓj(k)⁢(c) is given by

W=(w0(0)w1(0)⋯wn(0)w0(1)w1(1)⋯wn(1)⋮⋮⋱⋮w0(n)w1(n)⋯wn(n))=S⁢V−1.

Thus the kth row of W (starting with row 0) gives the weights for Dk⁢(f), and

(f⁢(c)f′⁢(c)⋮f(n)⁢(c))≈(D0⁢(f)D1⁢(f)⋮Dn⁢(f))=W⁢(f⁢(x0)f⁢(x1)⋮f⁢(xn)).
Proof.

Since each Lagrange polynomial ℓj has degree at most n, Taylor’s theorem gives

ℓj⁢(x)=∑k=0nℓj(k)⁢(c)k!⁢(x−c)k.

Let U be the matrix of these coefficients:

U=(ℓ0⁢(c)0!ℓ1⁢(c)0!⋯ℓn⁢(c)0!ℓ0′⁢(c)1!ℓ1′⁢(c)1!⋯ℓn′⁢(c)1!⋮⋮⋱⋮ℓ0(n)⁢(c)n!ℓ1(n)⁢(c)n!⋯ℓn(n)⁢(c)n!).

Evaluating the expansion at each node xi gives

(V⁢U)i⁢j=∑k=0n(xi−c)k⁢ℓj(k)⁢(c)k!=ℓj⁢(xi)=δi⁢j.

Thus V⁢U=I, so U=V−1. Multiplying row k of U by k! gives the weights wj(k)=ℓj(k)⁢(c), and so W=S⁢U=S⁢V−1. The vector formula follows from the definition of Dk⁢(f). ∎
